Kerf and slot fit: plan a joint that matches your sheet

Understand why measured thickness, cutting kerf and finishing affect the fit of interlocking laser-cut parts.

By PLYFORM · Practical planning guidance

Measure the actual sheet

Kerf is the width of material removed by a cut. Slot fit also depends on the actual sheet thickness, which may differ from the nominal label and vary across a sheet. Begin by measuring your material at representative points with an appropriate measuring tool.

Check which thickness the original file assumes. A design for one stock thickness may need adjusted slots for another. Resizing the entire artwork changes heights, lengths and holes together, so it is usually a different decision from changing only thickness-dependent joints.

Define the fit you need

A joint that must slide during use needs a different fit from a joint that is assembled once and glued. Decide whether the parts should move, disassemble or stay fixed. That intended behavior gives you a useful way to judge the sample.

Do not interpret a tight joint as automatically better. Excessive force can damage a narrow tab, distort an assembly or make a part difficult to replace. Look for a fit appropriate to the construction method described by the designer.

Make a representative fit sample

Create or use a small test containing the actual joint style at several nearby slot widths. Label the variations so you can identify the successful result. Keep the same material orientation and production workflow intended for the full build.

Cut the sample using your equipment’s documented workflow and inspect the result. The relationship between the drawn outline and the finished edge depends on how your software compensates for the cut, so verify the physical sample rather than copying a universal adjustment number.

Include the finish in your test

Coatings, sanding and glue can alter the final fit. Test the intended finishing order on at least one sample joint. Keep track of whether parts were fitted before or after coating so that the production workflow remains consistent.

For a large assembly, also check squareness and alignment. A single slot test cannot show whether several panels accumulate dimensional error. Dry-fit enough of the structure to understand its shape before fixing all the joints.

Document the working version

Save the chosen file revision with the measured thickness and the result of your sample. Make the record specific to the material and workflow you actually used. A useful note is more reliable than remembering that the stock was approximately a certain thickness.

Repeat the fit check if the stock changes or if the process changes enough to affect the cut. A well-fitting sample is a practical decision tool, not a guarantee that another sheet or machine will produce the same result.

Put the checklist into practice

Try the checklist against a box-making project, where slot fit matters, or a jewelry design, where small holes and fragile details need closer attention. Browse the model library only after you have a clear set of requirements.

Frequently asked questions

What is kerf?

Kerf is the width of material removed by a cut. Its effect on a joint must be evaluated with the actual cutting workflow and material.

Should I scale an entire file to change material thickness?

Usually inspect the thickness-dependent features separately. Whole-file scaling changes all dimensions and can affect hardware holes, clearances and finished size.
